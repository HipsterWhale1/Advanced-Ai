# Design Factors Underlying Immersive and Believable Video Game Experiences

## Academic Rewrite

### Introduction

Immersion and believability are central properties of interactive digital games, but neither property requires photorealistic representation or strict replication of the physical world. A fictional game world may be perceived as convincing when its rules are coherent, its environments communicate interpretable information, and its systems respond consistently to player input. These conditions enable players to construct a usable mental model of the game, make decisions within that model, and anticipate the consequences of their actions. Accordingly, an immersive and believable game experience should be understood as the product of interacting design systems rather than as the result of a single technical or aesthetic feature. The principal systems examined here are internal consistency and world rules, meaningful player agency, environmental storytelling and level design, environmental atmosphere and emotional tone, and plausible interactions supported by intuitive controls. In combination, these systems can produce a game world that players are able to interpret, explore, influence, and experience as emotionally significant.

### Internal Consistency and World Rules

Internal consistency and intelligible world rules provide a foundational condition for believability. A game need not reproduce reality with complete physical or social accuracy; rather, its physics, social structures, narrative events, and consequences must relate to one another in patterns that players can learn and anticipate. Weines and Borit identify consistency, completeness, correspondence, and coherent social or ecological systems as important characteristics of convincing game worlds. Hogenbirk, van de Hoef, and Meyer likewise argue that apparent inconsistencies are not necessarily detrimental when players can interpret them as established features of the game’s rule system rather than as contradictions. Consequently, rules must be sufficiently legible for players to identify possible actions and form reasonable expectations about their likely consequences.

Research by Cheng and Cairns complicates any assumption that inconsistency automatically eliminates immersion. Their findings indicate that players may remain immersed even when a game’s visual style and physics change inconsistently during play. This result suggests that a localized inconsistency does not necessarily destroy immersion, particularly after players have become engaged with the game. Nevertheless, repeated or confusing contradictions may undermine the perceived coherence of the world. A defensible design principle is therefore to combine rule clarity with opportunities for uncertainty. Players require enough information to establish expectations, while limited ambiguity can preserve exploration, interpretation, and discovery.

### Meaningful Player Agency and Persistent Consequences

Meaningful player agency emerges when players can express intentions through action and receive clear, interpretable responses from the game system. Agency should not be equated with the quantity of available choices. Cardona-Rivera et al. report that players experience stronger agency when choices produce meaningfully different situations, whereas Tanenbaum and Tanenbaum characterize agency as meaningful expression rather than unlimited freedom. Feedback is consequently necessary because players must be able to identify what changed and associate that change with their preceding actions. Guo and Lo identify feedback reasoning as a central component of perceived agency, and Koenitz, Roth, and Mekler found that explicit journal drawings communicated narrative consequences more effectively than an ambiguous in-game scale.

Persistent consequences can extend the experience of agency by allowing player-induced changes to remain visible in environments, characters, or subsequent events. Szolin et al.’s interview research associates persistent consequences with stronger attachment to player avatars, while Tranter, Hicks, and Headleand argue that visible persistence can reinforce engagement and immersion. Persistence alone, however, does not establish agency. Consequences must remain interpretable and sufficiently connected to the player’s decisions. Negative outcomes may also support meaningful agency when players understand their causes and retain a sense of autonomy, as indicated by research from Wright and Denisova and from Li and Jun on morally difficult choices. Because autonomy, meaning, and immersion represent distinct dimensions of player experience, meaningful agency should be treated as a strong, evidence-based contributor to immersion rather than as a universal guarantee of it.

### Environmental Storytelling and Convincing World and Level Design

Environmental storytelling and coherent level design can transform a game world from a collection of functional spaces into an interpretable and historically meaningful setting. Players use objects, architecture, landmarks, and spatial arrangements as evidence concerning how a world operates and what events have occurred within it. Smith Nicholls and Cook’s research on environmental affordances indicates that players construct interpretations by integrating visual evidence, available actions, spatial relationships, and prior knowledge. Fernández-Vara similarly explains that game spaces can communicate narrative information indirectly by presenting traces from which players infer events that are not directly represented.

Level design should guide exploration while preserving the player’s perception of discovery and choice. Hughes et al. find that open-world experiences benefit from connected spaces, meaningful content density, accessibility, and opportunities for self-paced exploration. Consequently, geographic scale alone does not establish immersion; scale must be supported by navigable structure and content that players perceive as relevant. Objects and environments can also communicate temporal development through decay, reconstruction, abandonment, and material evidence left by previous inhabitants. Zhang’s research on embedded narrative emphasizes that such discoveries should contribute to the larger narrative while remaining sufficiently discoverable for players to recognize their significance. Smith and Worch further argue that environmental clues are most effective when they prompt players to ask what occurred and develop conclusions independently.

Taken together, these findings support a design principle in which essential routes, affordances, and environmental information remain sufficiently readable to support navigation and interpretation, while optional narrative clues retain enough ambiguity to invite inference. This principle should not be treated as an experimentally validated rule that applies uniformly to every game. The appropriate relationship between clarity and ambiguity will depend on genre, narrative structure, accessibility requirements, and player expectations. Both qualities, however, can contribute to a world that is coherent, explorable, and meaningful.

### Environmental Atmosphere and Emotional Tone

Environmental atmosphere contributes to immersion by shaping the player’s emotional experience within a game world. Atmosphere is produced through the coordination of lighting, color, sound, silence, weather, environmental motion, composition, pacing, and visual style. Ribeiro et al.’s investigation of audiovisual thematic cohesion found that thematic conflict between sound and visual elements reduced perceived atmosphere, highlighting the importance of sensory consistency. Lighting and color may also influence both emotional response and practical engagement. Knez and Niedenthal found that warm lighting was associated with greater pleasantness and faster gameplay performance, while Geslin identified relationships between color properties, including brightness and saturation, and emotions such as fear, sadness, joy, and serenity.

These findings indicate that environmental design can shape emotional expectations and direct attention while also influencing the conditions under which players act. Atmosphere should not, however, be treated as synonymous with immersion, enjoyment, or presence. Ribeiro et al. found that audiovisual inconsistency reduced perceived atmosphere without necessarily reducing self-reported immersion. This distinction is important because a game may produce an immersive experience through mechanisms other than atmospheric cohesion, and atmospheric effects may not be uniform across players or contexts.

Believable visual design likewise need not be photorealistic. A stylized game can produce a convincing emotional world when its lighting, color, sound, environmental behavior, and compositional choices consistently support its artistic and thematic purposes. Effective atmosphere therefore depends on coordinated sensory choices that establish an appropriate emotional tone and provide the player with a coherent experiential context.

### Plausible Interactions and Intuitive Controls

Plausible interactions and intuitive controls connect player intentions to the game world and thereby make actions understandable and consequential. Believable interaction does not require complete physical accuracy. It requires interactive elements to respond in ways that players can predict and interpret. Pouke et al.’s research illustrates this distinction: in their experiments, participants often judged simplified “movie physics” to be more realistic than physically accurate behavior while interacting with objects at unusual scales. Their findings indicate that players evaluate realism according to expectations and contextual coherence rather than according to scientific accuracy alone.

Responsiveness is similarly important to embodied action. Normoyle and Jörg found that highly natural animation could reduce the promptness of character responses, whereas less natural movement sometimes produced greater satisfaction and a stronger sense of control. Schmierbach, Limperos, and Woolley found that a steering-wheel controller increased enjoyment in a racing game by making the control scheme feel more natural and by supporting transportation and an appropriate challenge-skill balance. These studies indicate that intuitive design requires coordination among natural mapping, responsive feedback, and consistent interaction rules. A realistic animation or physics system may weaken perceived plausibility if it delays, obscures, or contradicts the player’s intended action. Conversely, prompt controls and predictable interaction patterns can make the player’s actions feel integrated into the game world, thereby strengthening agency and contributing to immersion.

### Conclusion

An immersive and believable video game experience is best understood as the emergent result of several mutually reinforcing design systems. Consistent world rules allow players to develop expectations; meaningful choices and persistent consequences can strengthen responsibility when the game communicates how actions matter; and environmental storytelling and coherent level design make spaces readable, explorable, and suggestive of histories beyond direct observation. Coordinated atmosphere provides emotional significance through lighting, color, sound, pacing, and visual style, while plausible interactions and responsive controls connect player intentions to systemic outcomes.

These design conditions do not guarantee immersion for every player, because genre conventions, prior expectations, accessibility needs, and individual interpretation also influence experience. The available research nevertheless supports a coherent general conclusion: believability depends less on photorealism or perfect physical accuracy than on the consistency with which a game communicates its world and responds to its player. A game is therefore most likely to feel immersive when players can understand its rules, act meaningfully within them, interpret its spaces, respond emotionally to its atmosphere, and recognize that the world reacts coherently to their actions.
