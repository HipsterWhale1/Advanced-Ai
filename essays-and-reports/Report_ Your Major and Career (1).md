# AI in Game Development

I am studying computer science with the goal of pursuing a career in video game development. This essay focuses on how artificial intelligence has historically supported the design, development, and production of video games, how it is currently being used in real-world game development, and how I think AI will be used and how it will change game development over the next five years. 

### Past

Historically, artificial intelligence aided computer science by helping computers solve problems that normally required human reasoning. During the 1950s and 1960s, researchers used AI for logical reasoning, theorem proving, problem-solving, and early game-playing programs such as checkers and chess (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2025). Arthur Samuel’s checkers program was an early example of machine learning because it improved through experience rather than relying only on fixed instructions (Samuel, 1959). AI was also applied to software engineering through early systems designed to assist with debugging, program analysis, automated programming, and documentation (Massachusetts Institute of Technology, n.d.).

Within game development, early AI was used to control enemies and create opponents that responded to players through programmed rules and movement patterns. Games such as *Space Invaders* and *Pac-Man* demonstrated how simple decision-making could make computer-controlled enemies appear responsive (Hsu, 2020; Trouvé, 2024). By 1992, *Dune II: The Building of a Dynasty* had expanded game AI into large-scale strategic behavior. Its computer-controlled factions operated within systems involving resource gathering, base construction, army production, and attacks against opposing forces (Smith, 2021; Westwood Studios, 1992; Generation Amiga, 2026). *StarCraft* continued this development in 1998, using scripted AI that could follow build-and-expand strategies, harvest resources, produce units, and send organized attack waves (Blizzard Entertainment, n.d.; Rooijackers & Winands, 2017). Racing games used AI in different ways: *Mario Kart 64* used computer-controlled racers and rubber-banding mechanics that helped keep opponents near the player and maintain a competitive race (Glinert, 2010; Saltsman, 2009). As games moved into three-dimensional environments, developers increasingly used pathfinding and navigation systems to help characters move through complex spaces. Later, behavior trees helped organize and reuse more complicated NPC decision-making, while procedural content-generation techniques helped create levels, maps, terrain, encounters, and other game elements with less manual design work (Iovino et al., 2020; Risi & Togelius, 2020; Smith, 2015; Trouvé, 2024). These systems were generally scripted or rule-based rather than machine-learning systems, but they still represented important uses of AI because computers evaluated game conditions and selected actions according to goals (Blizzard Entertainment, n.d.; Generation Amiga, 2026).

AI and machine-learning techniques also contributed to the production, testing, and ongoing design of video games. Procedural generation continued to help developers create levels, terrain, items, environments, and encounters, while later research began combining procedural content generation with machine learning (Risi & Togelius, 2020). This approach could reduce manual design work and help smaller teams create larger or more varied game worlds; the independently developed *No Man’s Sky*, for example, used mathematical algorithms to generate an enormous universe of planets, terrain, and life forms (Khatchadourian, 2015). AI was also used to adapt gameplay to individual players. *Left 4 Dead* (2008) used an AI Director to adjust enemy encounters, supplies, and pacing according to the players’ performance and condition (Thue & Bulitko, 2012; Trouvé, 2024). In addition, researchers developed and evaluated AI-based approaches to automated playtesting, bug detection, level evaluation, and game balancing, although these methods were more often experimental or research-oriented than universally adopted across the industry (Albaghajati & Ahmed, 2023). Later research also explored machine-learning methods for generating and blending character motion from motion-capture data, potentially reducing the amount of animation that must be created manually (Zhao et al., 2024). Therefore, the historical role of AI in video game development extended beyond controlling NPCs: it helped developers generate content, construct interactive narratives, adapt gameplay, test games, and explore more efficient approaches to animation.

### Present

In the present day, artificial intelligence is increasingly integrated into video game development as a tool for creating code, assets, animations, environments, and gameplay systems (Game Developers Conference, 2025; Unity Technologies, 2024). On April 23, 2024, Unity announced that its Muse tools could operate directly inside the Unity Editor, helping developers troubleshoot errors, generate usable scripts, create sprites and textures, produce animation clips, and design character behaviors (Unity Technologies, 2024). AI is also being explored for procedural content generation, including levels, quests, characters, items, and environments that would otherwise require extensive manual design (Yang et al., 2025). The 2025 Game Developers Conference survey of more than 3,000 developers found that 52% worked for companies that had implemented generative AI and 36% personally used it (Game Developers Conference, 2025).

The current state of the art is moving beyond AI as a private development assistant toward systems that directly affect how players experience games. In March 2024, Ubisoft presented NEO NPCs, a player-facing prototype that used generative AI for unscripted dialogue, memory, contextual awareness, emotional reactions, animation, and collaborative decision-making; the system could also generate new quests during gameplay (Ubisoft, 2024). AI systems can also model player behavior and alter pacing, challenge, encounters, supplies, or available content to create a more individualized experience (Thue & Bulitko, 2012). NVIDIA’s ACE for Games provides developers with cloud and on-device models for speech, intelligence, and animation, with examples involving conversational characters, AI teammates, game advisors, and adaptive enemies (NVIDIA, n.d.). A 2025 review of GPT-related game research identified procedural content generation, mixed-initiative game design, mixed-initiative gameplay, game-playing systems, and game-user research as major application areas (Yang et al., 2025). Because Ubisoft described NEO NPCs as a prototype and recent research continues to study early adoption, these player-facing systems are best understood as frontier experimentation rather than standard features across commercial games (Ubisoft, 2024; Ternar et al., 2025).

The present use of AI in game development is also shaped by concerns about ownership, consent, transparency, privacy, and quality. Valve requires developers to disclose AI-generated content through Steam’s Content Survey and to describe safeguards for content generated while a game is running (Valve, n.d.). The U.S. Copyright Office has addressed digital replicas, the copyrightability of AI-generated material, and copyrighted training data through reports published between 2024 and 2025 (U.S. Copyright Office, 2025). In 2025, video game performers also negotiated protections requiring written consent for companies to create digital replicas of their voices or performances (Associated Press, 2025). Together, these developments suggest that AI is expanding game-production workflows and enabling new forms of gameplay, while developers and publishers must still verify outputs, protect creative workers, respect intellectual-property rights, and maintain human oversight (Game Developers Conference, 2025; Valve, n.d.; U.S. Copyright Office, 2025).

### Future

Although AI is already present in the development space, I think AI is going to become far more common and applicable in game development. Currently, it has a bit of a bad name; there are low-quality products, game developers trying to create something quickly for profit, and, as a result, the game-consuming community is learning to avoid slop. However, AI offers a huge increase in the amount of work that can be produced within a given amount of time, making work possible that might otherwise be impossible for a given team size or timeframe. In such a competitive market, AI is going to be used in every way possible to speed up production and increase the quality of work.

On the production side, I think 3D model generation will reach the level of current 2D generation, as will normal maps and textures. Asset creation, level generation, and everything visual will be heavily AI-assisted. Music and sounds will also be produced and adjusted by AI. I think AI will also play a massive part in brainstorming story, plot, and dialogue.

I think the next step is that AIs will be used *inside* games to generate all of the things mentioned above \- different assets and art for characters or locations, reorganized levels or even different levels entirely, even different locations and progression directions. We could see individualized stories for each playthrough, where each time would feel like a different game; although there are consistent rules and themes and concepts, no two playthroughs would be alike—not only because of procedural generation for terrain, as seen in current games, but also because of procedural generation for characters and locations, bosses and items, with perhaps even major plot points changing from run to run. Every person would have a different “version” of the same game.

AI will also be intimately used in programming, with a majority of player movement, rules, combat, and more becoming AI-created. AI will also be used for playtesting, finding bugs and exploits, and refining level progression, while humans define the architecture and requirements. I think AI tools will become integrated natively or through add-ons with all significant game engines, as we are already starting to see. I think even publicity and marketing, as well as conversing and connecting with an audience or community, could all be done by AI, with some degree of human oversight. Far in the future—more than five years from now—I could also imagine a game-modeled AI trained on tens of thousands of complete games that can generate a custom game for exactly what the user wants to play.

Overall, I think AI will become an integral part of the game development space, where it will be an indispensable resource needed to compete with other producers. They will play a major role in visuals and asset creation, story and dialogue, in-game dynamically creation of ever-unique NPCs, locations, and stories, to generating and testing code and systems, and even marketing and publicity, AI will be used in nearly every conceivable step of game development.
