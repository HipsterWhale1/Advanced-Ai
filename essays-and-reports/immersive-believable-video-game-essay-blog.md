# What Makes a Video Game Feel Real?

## A Blog-Style Look at Immersion and Believability

Have you ever played a game and suddenly realized that an hour had gone by? Maybe you were exploring a mysterious city, worrying about a character you had met only a few minutes earlier, or carefully planning your next move because you knew your choices would matter. That feeling is usually called immersion. A game does not have to look exactly like real life to create it. Even a game with cartoon graphics or imaginary creatures can feel believable if its world makes sense and reacts to the player in a consistent way.

The most immersive games usually combine several important features. Their rules are consistent, their choices have meaning, their environments tell stories, their atmosphere creates the right emotions, and their controls make actions feel natural. No single feature guarantees immersion for every player, but these parts work together to make a game world easier to understand, explore, influence, and care about.

## The World Needs Rules That Make Sense

The first step toward a believable game world is making sure the world follows rules that players can learn. Those rules do not have to be exactly like the rules of real life. A fantasy game can have magic, flying animals, or talking robots. What matters is that the game explains, directly or indirectly, how these things work and then follows its own rules.

Research by Weines and Borit suggests that convincing game worlds depend on qualities such as consistency, completeness, correspondence, and organized social or ecological systems. In simpler terms, the parts of the world should fit together. Hogenbirk, van de Hoef, and Meyer also point out that an apparent inconsistency is not always a problem. Players may accept something unusual if they understand it as part of the game’s established rules.

At the same time, players need to be able to recognize what they can do and what will probably happen next. Research by Cheng and Cairns shows that players can sometimes stay immersed even when a game changes its visual style or physics in inconsistent ways. This means that one strange moment does not automatically ruin a game. However, constant contradictions can make players stop trusting the world.

The best approach is often a balance between clarity and mystery. Players need enough information to form expectations, but they do not need every secret explained immediately. A world feels interesting when its rules are understandable while still leaving room for discovery.

## Choices Should Actually Matter

A game feels much more personal when players can make decisions that express what they want and then see the game respond. This is what meaningful player agency means. It is not simply about giving players a huge list of choices. The choices need to lead to different situations or communicate something about the player’s intentions.

Cardona-Rivera and colleagues found that players reported stronger agency when their decisions led to meaningfully different situations. Tanenbaum and Tanenbaum describe agency as meaningful expression rather than unlimited freedom. In other words, a game does not need to let players do everything. It needs to make the choices they can make feel important.

Feedback helps players understand that their decisions mattered. If a player changes something but never sees or hears any result, it is difficult to connect the outcome to the original choice. Guo and Lo identify this kind of feedback reasoning as an important part of agency. Koenitz, Roth, and Mekler also found that clear drawings in a game journal communicated story consequences more effectively than an unclear scale.

Consequences become even more powerful when they last. A changed environment, a character who remembers the player’s actions, or a later event caused by an earlier decision can make the game feel more connected. Research by Szolin and colleagues connects persistent consequences with stronger attachment to player avatars, while Tranter, Hicks, and Headleand argue that visible persistence can support engagement and immersion.

Still, lasting consequences do not automatically create agency. Players need to understand what caused the consequence. Even a negative result can feel meaningful if the player understands why it happened and still feels responsible for the decision. Research by Wright and Denisova and by Li and Jun supports the idea that difficult choices can matter when players understand their causes and retain a sense of autonomy. Meaningful agency can strongly support immersion, but it is not a guarantee that every player will feel immersed.

## Places Can Tell Stories Without Saying a Word

Some of the best storytelling in a game happens without a character explaining anything. A broken building, an abandoned camp, a locked door, or a trail of personal objects can make players wonder what happened. This is called environmental storytelling: using places and objects to communicate information about the world and its history.

Smith Nicholls and Cook found that players interpret environmental clues by combining what they see with the actions they can take, the arrangement of the space, and what they already know. Fernández-Vara explains that a game space can tell a story indirectly by leaving behind traces of events that the player never witnesses directly.

Level design also affects how players explore. A huge map is not automatically immersive. Research by Hughes and colleagues suggests that open-world games work better when spaces connect meaningfully, content is spread out in useful ways, areas are accessible, and players can explore at their own pace. Players should be guided enough to understand where they can go, but they should not feel as if every step has been forced.

Objects can also show the passage of time. Decay, rebuilding, abandoned belongings, and signs of previous inhabitants can make a location feel as if it existed before the player arrived. Zhang’s research on embedded narrative suggests that these discoveries should connect to the larger story while remaining discoverable enough for players to understand their importance. Smith and Worch argue that environmental clues are especially effective when they encourage players to ask questions and reach conclusions on their own.

This creates an important design balance. Main routes and basic actions should be easy enough to understand, while optional clues can be more mysterious. The exact balance will depend on the type of game and the expectations of its players, but both clarity and mystery can make a world feel meaningful.

## Atmosphere Makes a World Feel Emotional

A believable game world is not only a place that makes sense. It is also a place that makes the player feel something. Atmosphere comes from the combined effect of lighting, color, music, sound effects, silence, weather, movement, composition, and pacing.

Ribeiro and colleagues studied how well sound and visuals worked together. They found that when the sound suggested one theme while the visuals suggested another, players experienced less atmosphere. This shows why sensory consistency matters. If a scene is supposed to feel peaceful, bright music combined with frightening visuals might create confusion unless that conflict is intentional.

Lighting and color can affect both emotion and gameplay. Knez and Niedenthal found that warm lighting was connected with greater pleasantness and faster gameplay performance. Geslin found relationships between color features such as brightness and saturation and emotions including fear, sadness, joy, and serenity. These results suggest that visual design can influence what players expect to feel and where they focus their attention.

Atmosphere is not exactly the same thing as immersion, enjoyment, or presence. Ribeiro and colleagues found that inconsistent sound and visuals reduced the feeling of atmosphere without necessarily reducing players’ self-reported immersion. This means that atmosphere is one important part of the overall experience, but it is not the whole experience.

Photorealism is not required, either. A stylized game can create a convincing emotional world if its colors, lighting, sounds, and environmental behavior support the same artistic goal. A game can look simple and still feel emotionally powerful when all of its sensory choices work together.

## Controls Should Feel Natural and Responsive

The way players interact with a game also affects whether the world feels believable. Players do not need every action to follow real-world physics perfectly. They need the game to respond in ways they can predict and understand.

Pouke and colleagues demonstrated this idea in experiments involving objects at unusual scales. Participants sometimes judged simplified “movie physics” as more realistic than physically accurate behavior. This suggests that players judge realism based on their expectations and the context of the action, not only on scientific accuracy.

Responsiveness matters just as much as realism. Normoyle and Jörg found that very natural-looking animation could make character responses feel slower, while less natural movement sometimes produced greater satisfaction and a stronger sense of control. In a racing game, Schmierbach, Limperos, and Woolley found that a steering-wheel controller increased enjoyment because it made the controls feel more natural and supported the player’s sense of transportation and challenge.

These findings point to a useful balance. Controls should feel natural, but they also need to respond quickly. A realistic animation or physics system can weaken perceived plausibility if it delays or hides what the player tried to do. When actions happen promptly and follow understandable patterns, players are more likely to feel that their actions belong in the game world.

## The Big Picture

So, what makes a video game immersive and believable? The answer is not simply better graphics. A game becomes convincing when its different systems support one another. Consistent rules help players understand what to expect. Meaningful choices and lasting consequences make players feel responsible for what happens. Environmental storytelling and thoughtful level design make spaces interesting to explore. Atmosphere gives those spaces emotional meaning, and responsive controls connect the player’s intentions to the world.

These features will not affect every player in exactly the same way. Genre, personal expectations, accessibility needs, and individual interpretation all matter. Still, the research points to a clear general idea: believability depends more on coherence than on photorealism or perfect physical accuracy. Players are most likely to feel immersed when they can understand the world, act meaningfully within it, discover its stories, respond to its emotional atmosphere, and see the game react consistently to what they do.
