Part 3: Listening and Communicating Across Differences

Constructive communication begins with a realistic goal. When people discuss emotionally charged issues, they often assume the purpose must be to persuade the other person immediately. That assumption can make the conversation less productive. A better first goal is understanding: What does the other person believe? Why does the issue matter to them? What experiences, values, or concerns shape their position? Understanding does not require agreement. It simply means trying to represent another person’s view accurately before evaluating or criticizing it.

This distinction matters because disagreements are often experienced as personal threats. Political and moral beliefs can be connected to a person’s identity, family, community, religion, profession, or sense of fairness. If someone feels that an argument attacks their intelligence, character, or group, they may become defensive rather than reflective. The review by Jost, Baldassarri, and Druckman explains that people can defend both their own prior beliefs and the beliefs of groups with which they identify. In that situation, a disagreement about policy may feel like a disagreement about who the person is.

For this reason, listening is not a passive activity. Listening well requires attention, curiosity, and self-control. A person who is technically silent may still be preparing a rebuttal, searching for an inconsistency, or waiting for an opportunity to interrupt. Constructive listening requires temporarily setting aside the desire to win. The listener should ask questions that clarify the other person’s meaning rather than questions designed to trap them.

One useful technique is the paraphrase test. After someone explains a position, respond with: “Let me see if I understand you. You are concerned that…” Then ask: “Did I get that right?” This gives the speaker an opportunity to correct misunderstandings and prevents the listener from arguing against a position the other person does not actually hold. Braver Angels recommends listening to understand, asking curious questions, using “I” statements, and avoiding descriptions of the other side that depend on stereotypes or exaggeration.

“I” statements distinguish personal interpretation from universal certainty. Compare “Your side does not care about public safety” with “I see the policy differently because I am concerned about its effects on public safety.” The second statement still communicates disagreement, but it is less likely to assign a hostile motive to the other person.

Curiosity is another important skill. “What experience most influenced your view?” invites explanation. Good questions are open-ended, specific, and sincere rather than disguised accusations. It is also useful to ask whether the disagreement concerns facts, values, or the likely consequences of a policy.

Narrative exchange can help people communicate across differences. In three preregistered field experiments, Kalla and Broockman studied conversations about unauthorized immigrants and transgender people. Arguments alone did not change exclusionary attitudes, while similar conversations that included nonjudgmental personal narratives produced small but lasting reductions in exclusionary attitudes.

Empathy means trying to understand another person’s experience, emotions, or perspective. It does not require accepting their conclusion, excusing harmful behavior, or abandoning boundaries. Moral reframing connects an argument to values the listener already considers important. In a field experiment about abortion, canvassers listened for individual voters’ moral values and tailored appeals to those values. The conversations affected political action and showed some evidence of attitude change.

Moral reframing must remain honest. It should not mean pretending to share a value one does not hold or hiding the real purpose of a conversation. Emotional regulation also matters. A pause can be a communication skill rather than a failure. Someone might say, “I want to continue this conversation, but I am becoming too frustrated to listen well.” Ending a conversation is appropriate when it becomes abusive, threatening, or unproductive.

Constructive disagreement does not require endless conversation or guaranteed compromise. Some disagreements concern basic rights or personal safety, where asking the vulnerable person to compromise may be unjust. The purpose of listening is to understand the disagreement accurately, communicate one’s own position honestly, and create a reasonable opportunity for reflection when both people are willing to participate in good faith.

The central principle was: “Understand the person’s position accurately before trying to change it—and remember that understanding is not the same as agreement.”

Part 5: Institutional Approaches to Constructive Dialogue

Constructive dialogue is not only an individual skill. Governments, schools, universities, public-health organizations, and media institutions help determine what information people encounter, whom they trust, and whether disagreement becomes productive or hostile. Institutions cannot force people to become open-minded, but they can create conditions that make careful thinking, respectful interaction, and voluntary belief revision more likely.

A useful starting point is to distinguish misinformation, disinformation, partisan animosity, and support for antidemocratic practices or political violence. These problems may overlap, but they are not identical. An institution that reduces hostility may not reduce misinformation, and an institution that improves factual knowledge may not change moral commitments.

The Science megastudy tested 25 interventions with more than 32,000 Americans. Many interventions reduced partisan animosity, especially those highlighting common identities or sympathetic people with different political views. Other interventions were more useful for reducing support for undemocratic practices, including correcting misperceptions about political opponents and emphasizing the danger of democratic collapse. Different democratic problems may require different interventions.

Governments and public-health organizations should communicate accurately, understandably, consistently, and honestly about uncertainty. The National Academies recommends trusted messengers, personally relevant information, attention to reference groups, and direct responses to misinformation. Institutions should avoid humiliating people for earlier decisions. Instead, they can explain that new evidence or circumstances are now available and that a reasonable person might update a decision.

Public institutions should explain how evidence was produced and distinguish preliminary reports, observational studies, randomized experiments, and broad scientific consensus. In vaccine communication, unverified reports should be distinguished from safety signals supported by rigorous investigation.

Schools and universities can teach source evaluation, uncertainty, media literacy, structured listening, paraphrasing, intellectual humility, and respectful disagreement. Students can learn to ask who produced information, what evidence and methods were used, what would count against a claim, and whether a source is reporting, interpreting, or advocating. Structured discussion is generally safer and more educational than unmoderated confrontation.

Constructive dialogue does not require treating every position as equally credible or appropriate for debate. Schools need to balance openness with protection against harassment, threats, and dehumanization. Academic freedom does not require educators to provide a platform for every claim in every setting.

Media organizations influence polarization through what they report, how they frame it, whom they quote, and how much attention they give conflict. UNESCO emphasizes verification, fact-checking, source evaluation, and media literacy. Responsible media should distinguish reporting from commentary, explain the strength and limitations of evidence, and avoid false balance. False balance presents two positions as equally supported even when the evidence strongly favors one.

Media organizations should be cautious about repeating false claims. Repetition can increase familiarity, but the evidence does not support the simple idea that every correction backfires. Corrections can work when they come from credible sources, clearly explain the error, and provide a coherent alternative account.

Trust develops through competence, transparency, consistency, and accountability. Institutions should acknowledge mistakes, explain changes in guidance, disclose conflicts of interest, and make evidence accessible. A technically expert national agency may lack local credibility, so institutional communication may work better when it combines expertise with trusted, relatable messengers.

Institutions should measure specific outcomes rather than simply claiming to promote “better dialogue.” Possible outcomes include factual knowledge, reduced misinformation, willingness to listen, lower partisan hostility, increased trust, reduced support for political violence, cooperation, or more accurate evaluation of evidence. The Science megastudy shows that interventions reducing partisan animosity were not necessarily the same interventions reducing antidemocratic attitudes.

The central lesson was: “Institutions cannot command open-mindedness, but they can design environments that reward accuracy, transparency, respectful contact, and voluntary reflection.”
