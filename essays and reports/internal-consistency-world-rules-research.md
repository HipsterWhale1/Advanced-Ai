# Internal Consistency and World Rules

## Research reference for future essay

**Essay context:** What makes an immersive and believable video game?

**Working subtopic:** Internal Consistency and World Rules

**Research question:** How do consistent rules shape what players expect, understand, and emotionally accept about a game world?

## Working thesis for this subtopic

A believable game does not need to imitate reality perfectly or explain every aspect of its world. It needs to establish rules that players can learn, interpret, and use to anticipate consequences, while allowing enough ambiguity and emergence for players to construct meaning themselves.

## Important source-use note

This document preserves the research synthesis and is not a substitute for consulting the original articles. Several sources were available as complete open-access papers, while others were accessible primarily through abstracts, institutional records, or publisher summaries. ## Core source summaries

### 1. Cheng and Cairns, “Behaviour, Realism and Immersion in Games”

- **Type:** Primary source; experimental study.
- **Finding:** The authors changed a game's visual style and physics midway through play with 14 participants. Most participants did not notice the inconsistency, suggesting that immersion can sometimes persist despite a mismatch between visual and behavioral realism.
- **Use:** Supports the nuanced claim that inconsistency does not automatically destroy immersion, especially after engagement has already formed.

### 2. Hogenbirk, van de Hoef, and Meyer, “Clarifying Incoherence in Games”

- **Type:** Secondary source; philosophical analysis.
- **Finding:** Using examples such as Mario dying and then respawning, the authors argue that incoherence is not simply an objective contradiction in a fictional world. It depends on how players interpret the relationship between game rules and fictional events.
- **Expertise evidence:** The authors are affiliated with Utrecht University; Meyer is a professor of artificial intelligence, and van de Hoef is a PhD candidate in interdisciplinary game research.
- **Use:** Supports the claim that player interpretation is central to whether an inconsistency is experienced as a problem.

### 3. Weines and Borit, “Better Game Worlds by Design”

- **Type:** Secondary source; design and analysis framework.
- **Finding:** The GAS framework identifies invention, completeness, consistency, and correspondence with real or fictional systems as properties of credible game worlds. Contradictions in story, infrastructure, or mechanics can make a world feel random or disconnected.
- **Expertise evidence:** Weines is a game-studies PhD researcher, and Borit is an associate professor specializing in social simulation, autonomous agents, and game-based learning.
- **Use:** Provides a practical framework for analyzing whether a game world functions as a coherent system.

### 4. Ang, “Rules, Gameplay, and Narratives in Video Games”

- **Type:** Secondary source; theoretical analysis.
- **Finding:** Ang distinguishes an abstract rules layer from a narrative layer and argues that the two should complement rather than contradict one another.
- **Expertise evidence:** Ang is a professor of human-computer interaction at the University of Kent whose research includes game and immersive technologies.
- **Use:** Useful for discussing rule–narrative alignment and ludonarrative dissonance.

### 5. Zaini, Fowler, Amor, and Wünsche, “Character-Driven Storytelling Design for Digital Games”

- **Type:** Secondary source; scoping review of 69 studies.
- **Finding:** Consistent motivations, actions, emotional responses, visual presentation, and player choices strengthen character believability. False choices and uncharacteristic behavior can weaken narrative immersion.
- **Expertise evidence:** The authors work in game design, human-computer interaction, computer science, and visual computing.
- **Use:** Extends internal consistency from world rules to character and narrative logic.

## Additional source summaries

### Rule legibility

### 6. Andersen et al., “The Impact of Tutorials on Games of Varying Complexity”

- **Type:** Primary source; large-scale player study.
- **Finding:** The researchers tested eight tutorial designs in three games with more than 45,000 players. Tutorials improved engagement in the most complex game but had little effect in simpler games, suggesting that rule instruction should match mechanical complexity and that some systems are better learned through experimentation.
- **Use:** Supports the idea that consistent rules must also be legible to players, but that legibility does not always require explicit explanation.

### 7. Cao and Liu, “Learning to Play: Understanding In-Game Tutorials with a Pilot Study on Implicit Tutorials”

- **Type:** Primary source; literature review plus pilot study of 47 players.
- **Finding:** Practice, timely feedback, and just-in-time guidance help players learn mechanics. Implicit, integrated tutorials may be especially useful for experienced players.
- **Use:** Supports the importance of feedback and context-sensitive rule teaching.

### Emergent edge cases

### 8. “Exploring How Players Use Emergent Narrative in Strategy Games”

- **Type:** Primary source; player-focused study of emergent narrative in the *Total War* series.
- **Finding:** Players use unexpected events and system interactions to construct personal stories about their gameplay. Emergent events can become meaningful when players connect them to their decisions and the game's underlying systems.
- **Use:** Supports the argument that unexpected outcomes can strengthen believability rather than undermine it.

### 9. Soler-Adillon, “The Open, the Closed and the Emergent: Theorizing Emergence for Videogame Studies”

- **Type:** Secondary source; theoretical analysis.
- **Finding:** Simple rules can generate complex, unplanned situations. Emergence is not automatically a design failure; it can enrich the experience when unexpected results arise from understandable systems rather than arbitrary glitches.
- **Expertise evidence:** Soler-Adillon is an associate professor at the Universitat Oberta de Catalunya whose work focuses on interactive systems, behavior design, and audience participation.
- **Use:** Helps distinguish productive emergence from incoherence or bugs.

### Temporal persistence

### 10. Gustafsson, Holme, and Mackay, “Play Arcs”

- **Type:** Primary source; MMO technology probe tested with 49 players.
- **Finding:** The authors developed “history game mechanics” that preserved player-created histories. They found that persistent histories could produce coherent stories and guide later decisions, allowing the game world to feel as though it remembers past actions.
- **Use:** Supports the importance of lasting consequences and world memory.

### 11. Gustafsson et al., “Narrative Substrates”

- **Type:** Primary source; four studies and a prototype deployed over two iterations across one year.
- **Finding:** Players valued unique events, and preserving traces of player activity could create more meaningful interactive stories. Persistence could also disrupt gameplay if past actions were not organized or managed carefully.
- **Use:** Shows both the value and design risks of persistent consequences.

### Completeness versus mystery

### 12. Van de Mosselaer and Gualeni, “The Fictional Incompleteness of Digital Gameworlds”

- **Type:** Secondary source; theoretical analysis.
- **Finding:** Game worlds are necessarily incomplete because players encounter inaccessible areas, repeated assets, limited affordances, and visible boundaries. Incompleteness does not automatically damage immersion; its effect depends on how players interpret it.
- **Expertise evidence:** Van de Mosselaer is an assistant professor at Tilburg University, and Gualeni is a professor at the University of Malta specializing in game studies, virtual worlds, and interactive philosophy.
- **Use:** Provides the conceptual distinction between unavoidable incompleteness and immersion-breaking artificiality.

### 13. Andriano, “Enjoying the Uncertainty”

- **Type:** Secondary source; peer-reviewed case analysis.
- **Finding:** Through an analysis of *Dark Souls*, Andriano argues that ambiguity and missing information can be engaging when incompleteness is consistently built into narrative, mechanics, and level design. Mystery works best when uncertainty follows a recognizable design pattern rather than appearing random.
- **Expertise evidence:** The article appears in the peer-reviewed journal *Games and Culture*, and Andriano is affiliated with the Department of Philosophy at the University of Milan.
- **Use:** Supports the argument that a believable world can remain mysterious without being incoherent.

## Points of agreement

1. **Consistency matters more than literal realism.** A game can depart from real-world physics or social rules if its fictional rules are stable and understandable.

2. **Player interpretation is central.** Believability is not only an objective feature of the game; it depends on how players interpret rules, conventions, exceptions, and consequences.

3. **Rules must be learnable.** Players need ways to discover the rules through tutorials, feedback, practice, experimentation, or observation.

4. **Rules should produce meaningful consequences.** Persistent effects help players feel that their actions matter and that the world has continuity.

5. **Emergence can strengthen immersion.** Unexpected outcomes can feel alive and believable when they arise from consistent systems.

6. **Incompleteness and ambiguity can be productive.** A world does not need to explain everything if its mystery is intentional and follows a recognizable pattern.

7. **Different layers of design should reinforce one another.** Mechanics, narrative, interface, audiovisual presentation, characters, and world systems should communicate compatible expectations.

## Points of disagreement or tension

### Whether inconsistency damages immersion

Weines and Borit, Ang, and Zaini et al. emphasize that contradictions can weaken believability. Cheng and Cairns found that participants often remained immersed despite a major change in physics and visual behavior.

The most plausible reconciliation is that inconsistency is most damaging when it occurs before immersion has formed, affects a central rule, or prevents the player from understanding what to do. Minor inconsistencies may be ignored after the player becomes deeply engaged.

### Whether players should be explicitly taught the rules

Andersen et al. find that tutorials may be unnecessary in simple games, while Cao and Liu emphasize the benefits of practice and timely guidance. Together, they suggest that explicit instruction is most valuable for complex mechanics, while simple systems may be better learned through experimentation.

### Whether completeness is necessary

Weines and Borit treat completeness as one factor that helps a world feel credible. Van de Mosselaer and Gualeni and Andriano emphasize that incompleteness can be unavoidable or desirable.

The useful distinction is between unexplained incompleteness and purposeful incompleteness. A world can remain mysterious while still being internally coherent.

### Whether designers should control or encourage unexpected outcomes

The worldbuilding frameworks emphasize consistency checks and deliberate rule design. The emergence sources argue that designers should allow systems to produce outcomes they did not explicitly script.

The strongest synthesis is that designers should control the underlying rules while allowing players to create uncontrolled combinations of those rules.

### Whether persistence always improves immersion

The persistence sources support lasting consequences but also identify risks. Persistent changes can create meaningful histories, yet they can confuse players, disrupt later content, or become difficult to manage when too many past events accumulate.

## Currently unknown

- There is no established threshold for how many contradictions players will tolerate.
- Researchers do not yet know which inconsistencies players notice most strongly.
- The causal relationship between consistency and immersion remains under-tested.
- Findings may not generalize across genres, platforms, cultures, or multiplayer contexts.
- The field lacks a single accepted measure of rule legibility.
- The boundary between productive emergence and accidental bugs remains unclear.
- The ideal amount of persistence is unknown; too little may make choices feel meaningless, while too much may overwhelm the player or create contradictions.
- Researchers have not established when mystery becomes confusion or frustration.
- More research is needed on how player expertise changes tolerance for ambiguity and inconsistency.

## Synthesis for the future essay

Internal consistency should be presented as a balance between **clarity and openness**. If rules are too obscure, players become confused; if every rule and outcome is explicitly explained, the world may feel mechanical. The strongest design establishes a stable underlying system while leaving room for emergence, mystery, and personal interpretation.

### Possible paragraph-level claim

An immersive and believable game does not need to reproduce reality or explain every detail. Instead, it must give players a coherent system of rules that they can learn, apply, and use to predict consequences, while allowing ambiguity and emergent events to create discovery and personal meaning.

Before drafting the essay, return to the original articles for exact quotations, methods, sample sizes, limitations, and distinctions between immersion, believability, agency, narrative coherence, and player engagement.

# Meaningful Player Agency and Persistent Consequences

## Research reference for future essay

**Combined subtopic:** Meaningful Player Agency and Persistent Consequences

**Working research question:** To what extent can players express intentions through their actions and observe those actions producing lasting changes in the game world, story, relationships, or avatar?

## Working thesis for this subtopic

Meaningful player agency does not require unlimited freedom. It requires that players can express intentions, recognize the game's response, and see those responses produce consequences that matter within the continuing life of the game world.

## Source summaries

### 1. Thue et al., “A Computational Model of Perceived Agency in Video Games”

- **Type:** Primary source; 141-participant user study.
- **Finding:** The researchers tested adaptive story events designed to increase perceived agency. Control and experimental groups received the same overall amount of agency, but the experimental group reported greater agency when the system selected outcomes that better matched players' preferences.
- **Use:** Demonstrates that agency is partly perceptual: players may feel more control when game consequences are desirable and appear connected to their intentions.

### 2. Cardona-Rivera et al., “Foreseeing Meaningful Choices”

- **Type:** Primary source; experiment with 88 participants.
- **Finding:** Participants played a choose-your-own-adventure game and reported greater agency when choices led to meaningfully different situations rather than merely different surface details.
- **Use:** Supports the claim that choices feel meaningful when players can anticipate that they will produce different states of the story or world. It also emphasizes that both immediate feedback and long-term significance matter.

### 3. Szolin, Kuss, Nuyens, and Griffiths, “I Am the Character, the Character Is Me”

- **Type:** Primary source; semi-structured interviews with 12 players from the United Kingdom and United States.
- **Finding:** Participants described stronger avatar attachment when their actions had observable effects on the avatar or game world. Difficulty, customization, and responsive controls also influenced the user–avatar relationship.
- **Use:** Shows how consequences connect the player's decisions to the avatar's identity and fate. Because the sample was small and qualitative, it provides detailed perspectives rather than broadly generalizable causal evidence.

### 4. Gustafsson et al., “Narrative Substrates: Reifying and Managing Emergent Narratives in Persistent Game Worlds”

- **Type:** Primary source; four studies and a prototype deployed over two iterations across one year.
- **Finding:** Players valued unique events, and preserving traces of player activity could transform past actions into future story content. The research also found that persistence can disrupt gameplay if past actions are not organized, represented, or integrated into later content effectively.
- **Use:** Provides direct evidence for the value and design risks of persistent consequences.

### 5. Tanenbaum and Tanenbaum, “Commitment to Meaning: A Reframing of Agency in Games”

- **Type:** Secondary source; theoretical analysis.
- **Finding:** The authors argue that agency should not be defined only as freedom or the number of available choices. Instead, it is the player's ability to express an intention or commitment and receive a meaningful response from the game; even constrained actions can feel agentic if the game recognizes their significance.
- **Expertise evidence:** The authors were affiliated with Simon Fraser University's School of Interactive Arts and Technology. Theresa Tanenbaum later became an associate professor of informatics at the University of California, Irvine, and is an established game designer and interactive-narrative scholar.
- **Use:** Provides a broader conceptual definition of agency that includes meaningful expression, communication, and response rather than unrestricted choice.

## Points of agreement

1. **Agency depends on meaningful responses, not merely available choices.** Players need to feel that their choices express an intention and produce a response that matters.

2. **Consequences must be discernible.** Players need to recognize the connection between an action and its result, whether the result is immediate or delayed.

3. **Persistent consequences strengthen meaning.** Lasting changes to the story, world, avatar, relationships, or future opportunities make actions feel more important.

4. **Perceived agency matters as much as actual agency.** A tightly authored game may still feel agentic if it provides meaningful feedback and aligns outcomes with player intentions.

5. **Agency is compatible with constraint.** Every game limits what players can do; the design challenge is to make those constraints feel purposeful rather than arbitrary.

## Points of disagreement or tension

### Actual change versus perceived agency

Cardona-Rivera et al. emphasize choices that lead to genuinely different situational outcomes, and Gustafsson et al. emphasize real persistence and lasting world changes. Thue et al., however, show that perceived agency can increase even when players receive the same underlying amount of agency.

This creates a distinction between actual agency and experienced agency: real change may be necessary for some forms of agency, but perception is also shaped by feedback, desirability, and interpretation.

### Freedom versus meaningful expression

Tanenbaum and Tanenbaum explicitly reject the idea that agency is primarily freedom or unrestricted choice. The empirical sources use a more conventional definition involving influence over events and outcomes, so the sources differ mainly in emphasis: one is philosophical and communicative, while the others are operational and measurable.

### Desirable outcomes versus understandable outcomes

Thue et al.'s model predicts greater agency when players receive outcomes they desire. The other sources focus more on meaningful difference and consequence than on whether outcomes are positive. The research does not yet establish whether players need satisfying outcomes or merely understandable ones to feel agency.

### The value and risk of persistence

The sources support persistent consequences, but Gustafsson et al. also identify risks. Persistent history can create richer stories while simultaneously confusing players, disrupting later content, or increasing the system's design burden.

## Currently unknown

- How much actual change is required for meaningful agency.
- Whether perceived agency can compensate for limited real agency over long periods.
- Whether desirable outcomes are necessary, or whether clearly connected negative outcomes can feel equally agentic.
- How long a consequence must persist to matter.
- Which forms of persistence matter most: geography, dialogue, factions, inventory, character development, narrative memory, or visual change.
- How players learn that a consequence was caused by them rather than by random events or scripted progression.
- How player personality, expertise, and genre expectations affect agency.
- How well these findings generalize beyond narrative games to simulations, strategy games, sandboxes, multiplayer games, and competitive games.
- Whether persistent consequences improve immersion, enjoyment, attachment, or replayability independently.
- How developers can manage large amounts of persistent history without producing contradictions or overwhelming players.

## Synthesis for the future essay

The strongest design principle is a responsiveness-and-consequence loop:

**player intention → game response → observable consequence → changed future possibilities**

When this loop is clear and persistent, players are more likely to feel responsible for the world and emotionally invested in what happens next.

## Source-use reminder

Before drafting, return to the original articles for exact quotations, methods, sample sizes, limitations, and distinctions between actual agency, perceived agency, immersion, attachment, enjoyment, and persistent world change.

# Environmental Storytelling and Convincing World/Level Design

## Scope and five-part framework

This general subtopic combines Environmental Storytelling with Convincing World and Level Design. The current framework has five connected parts:

1. **Players interpreting environmental clues** — affordances, readability, visual signposting, landmarks, environmental information, and how players infer meaning from spaces and objects.
2. **Level design guiding exploration and action** — spatial logic, navigational coherence, world scale, content density, route choice, pacing, accessibility, and self-paced discovery.
3. **Objects and spaces communicating past events** — environmental storytelling, temporal layering, traces of history, decay, reconstruction, and material evidence of previous events.
4. **Environmental atmosphere and emotional tone** — lighting, color, sound, weather, composition, pacing, audiovisual cohesion, and visual coherence.
5. **Cross-disciplinary consistency** — a recurring design requirement: narrative, art, audio, level design, and gameplay systems should collectively support the same world and emotional experience.

## Complete source set and findings

### 1. Smith Nicholls and Cook, “Archaeological Gameworld Affordances: A Grounded Theory of How Players Interpret Environmental Storytelling”

**Primary source.** A peer-reviewed ACM conference study using a procedurally generated ruined village and data from 202 participants. It examines how players interpret environmental storytelling through visual detail, affordances, spatial arrangement, and prior knowledge. Players construct possible explanations from environmental evidence rather than simply decoding one fixed message.

### 2. Karlsson, Brusk, and Engström, “Level Design Processes and Challenges”

**Primary source.** A fieldwork and interview study of professional game developers. It emphasizes interdisciplinary coordination, communication, testing, pacing, navigation, and the integration of gameplay with narrative and visual goals. Convincing level design is both a spatial problem and an organizational problem.

### 3. Hughes et al., “Opening the World of Contextually-Specific Player Experiences”

**Primary source.** A study of open-world players using interviews and player commentary. It identifies large but connected spaces, accessibility, appropriate content density, nonrestrictive goals, and self-paced discovery as important to meaningful open-world exploration. A large map alone does not create immersion; scale must be supported by meaningful content and navigational understanding.

### 4. Zhang, “Best Practice: Implementing Embedded Narrative to Enhance Storytelling”

**Primary source.** A design-based research project that developed and evaluated a game level inspired by *Dying Light*. It recommends supporting the main narrative, controlling pacing, making information discoverable, and encouraging engagement. Environmental narrative works best when players discover story through action and exploration rather than receiving it only through exposition.

### 5. Fernández-Vara, “Game Spaces Speak Volumes: Indexical Storytelling”

**Secondary source.** Fernández-Vara is a recognized game-studies scholar whose research focuses on game narrative, environmental storytelling, and the relationship between space and interpretation. The article explains how objects, spatial arrangements, traces, and environmental changes function as evidence from which players construct stories.

### 6. Wei and Wang, “From Construction to Perception: Three Views of Level Design for Story-Driven Games”

**Secondary source.** This peer-reviewed design-research chapter examines level design as construction, player perception, and narrative experience. It argues that level design shapes first impressions, ongoing perceptions, routes, actions, and interpretations; meaning emerges through the interaction between designed structure and player experience.

### 7. Weines and Borit, “Better Game Worlds by Design”

**Secondary source.** This theoretical framework proposes that believable game worlds require invention, completeness, consistency, correspondence, and coherent social and ecological systems. A world need not reproduce reality exactly; it needs internal relationships among its history, institutions, ecologies, resources, and social systems.

### 8. Smith and Worch, “What Happened Here? Environmental Storytelling”

**Secondary source.** This professional design lecture explains how environmental storytelling is created through props, lighting, composition, textures, scripted events, and environmental reactions. The strongest environments prompt players to ask what happened and then investigate clues rather than receiving every event through direct explanation.

### 9. Ribeiro et al., “Game Atmosphere: Effects of Audiovisual Thematic Cohesion on Player Experience and Psychophysiology”

**Primary source.** A peer-reviewed CHI PLAY study with two experiments and 109 participants. It found that poor thematic fit between audio and visuals reduced perceived atmosphere, although audiovisual inconsistency did not necessarily reduce self-reported immersion. Atmosphere and immersion overlap but are not identical.

### 10. Knez and Niedenthal, “Lighting in Digital Game Worlds: Effects on Affect and Play Performance”

**Primary source.** An experiment comparing warm and cool lighting in digital game worlds. Warm lighting produced greater pleasantness and was associated with faster and better performance, suggesting that lighting can affect both emotional tone and the player’s ability to act comfortably within a space.

### 11. Geslin, “How Color Properties Can Be Used to Elicit Emotions in Video Games”

**Primary source.** An empirical study with 85 participants and 24 video-game environment images. It found relationships between hue, brightness, saturation, saliency, and emotions such as joy, sadness, fear, and serenity, and proposes color scripting as a method for shaping emotional changes across an environment. Individual interpretation still varies.

## Points of broad agreement

- Believability depends more on coherent relationships than on photorealism alone.
- Players actively interpret environments rather than merely receiving information.
- Readability and affordances help players understand what matters, what is usable, and where action is possible.
- Large spaces require meaningful density, landmarks, connections, and navigational structure.
- Environmental storytelling benefits from implication and discovery, but clues must remain sufficiently legible.
- Narrative, art, audio, level design, and gameplay systems should reinforce one another.
- Atmosphere is produced through coordinated sensory cues, including lighting, color, sound, composition, and pacing.

## Points of disagreement or qualification

- The sources differ on how much information should be given directly. Environmental-storytelling scholarship values inference and ambiguity, while embedded-narrative design emphasizes discoverability and support for the main narrative.
- Ambiguity can encourage curiosity, but excessive ambiguity can prevent players from understanding available actions or intended meanings.
- Ribeiro et al. show that audiovisual mismatch weakens perceived atmosphere without necessarily weakening self-reported immersion, complicating any simple equation between atmosphere and immersion.
- Visual atmosphere has both expressive and functional roles: some sources emphasize mood, while others emphasize navigation, performance, and action.
- Larger worlds are not automatically better; meaningful density and coherence can matter more than total size.
- Color and lighting can guide emotional response, but they cannot guarantee a universal interpretation because players bring different experiences and expectations.

## Currently unknown

- How much environmental information players can process before spaces become cluttered or over-explained.
- Which landmarks and spatial cues work best across genres, cultures, and skill levels.
- How players distinguish intentional clues from decorative detail.
- How environmental storytelling changes for players with different abilities, cultural backgrounds, or genre familiarity.
- How atmosphere develops over long play sessions rather than short experiments.
- Which combination of sound, lighting, color, weather, animation, and pacing produces the strongest atmosphere.
- When ambiguity encourages curiosity and when it becomes confusion.
- How designers should measure whether players understood an environmental story correctly.
- How much cross-disciplinary coordination is necessary before additional coordination has diminishing returns.
- How persistent world changes and temporal traces affect long-term attachment to a place.

## Notes for the five subsections

### Players interpreting environmental clues

Focus on how players read spaces as evidence. Include affordances, readability, visual signposting, landmarks, object placement, spatial relationships, contextual knowledge, and the difference between authorial intention and player interpretation. The central claim is that environmental storytelling succeeds when players can form plausible interpretations without being forced into one literal reading.

### Level design guiding exploration and action

This section absorbs “Open-world scale, density, and self-paced discovery.” Include spatial logic, navigational coherence, landmarks, memorable routes, world size relative to meaningful content, density versus emptiness, accessibility, pacing, and the balance between guidance and freedom. Exploration feels believable when the world seems large enough to exist beyond the player but structured enough to remain understandable and worth exploring.

### Objects and spaces communicating past events

This section absorbs “Temporal layering and traces of history.” Include decay, repair, abandonment, reconstruction, objects left by former inhabitants, environmental changes, old and new construction, evidence of conflict or everyday life, and gaps between what happened and what the player directly witnesses. Players should be able to reconstruct a plausible past from material traces rather than relying only on dialogue or exposition.

### Environmental atmosphere and emotional tone

This is the highlighted section. Cover lighting, color, soundscapes, silence, weather, animation, composition, audiovisual thematic cohesion, emotional pacing, appropriate realism, and stylistic consistency. Atmosphere emerges from coordinated sensory and spatial choices; photorealism is unnecessary if the artistic style and environmental behavior support a consistent emotional and thematic experience.

### Cross-disciplinary consistency

Keep this as a recurring principle rather than a separate major body section. It includes coordination among narrative, art, audio, level design, and systems teams; shared rules for history and tone; consistency between story claims and environmental evidence; consistency between visual cues and gameplay affordances; and testing whether the combined signals are interpreted as intended. Convincing environmental storytelling is produced by coordinated decisions that make spaces, objects, sensory cues, history, and gameplay actions feel like parts of the same place.

The strongest overall claim for this subtopic is that immersive environmental design emerges when players can understand, explore, emotionally experience, and reconstruct the history of a world whose narrative, visual, audio, spatial, and gameplay systems remain mutually coherent.

# Plausible Interactions and Physics / Intuitive Controls and Embodied Action

## Research reference for future essay

This smaller section examines how believable interactions depend on expected coherence, responsiveness, and understandable mappings between player input and game-world action. The central distinction is between realism as accurate simulation and realism as experienced plausibility.

## Source summaries

### 1. Pouke et al., “The Plausibility Paradox for Resized Users in Virtual Environments”

**Primary source.** This peer-reviewed *Frontiers in Virtual Reality* article reports two original experiments involving 84 participants. Participants interacted with objects under either physically accurate “true physics” or simplified “movie physics.”

Participants often judged the simplified physics as more realistic than scientifically accurate physics, especially when the virtual user was scaled down. In one experiment, 73% considered the movie-physics condition more realistic, and 91% said it better matched their expectations.

The central finding is that believable physics depends on expected coherence, not necessarily objective physical accuracy. Players may prefer interactions that follow familiar conventions and intuitive expectations, especially when unusual scale or perspective makes real-world physics counterintuitive. Accurate physics can still help communicate an altered sense of body scale, showing that perceived realism and embodied plausibility are not identical.

### 2. Normoyle and Jörg, “The Effect of Animation Controller and Avatar on Player Perceptions”

**Primary source.** This peer-reviewed study conducted three experiments in a simple platform game, comparing animation naturalness, responsiveness, and avatar models.

The least responsive controller reduced player performance and perceived control. However, the most natural-looking controller did not necessarily produce the best player experience. Participants could be more satisfied with their performance when using a less natural controller, while animation differences still affected enjoyment even when responsiveness remained constant.

The study demonstrates a trade-off between lifelike movement and immediate control. Players may prefer animation that looks less realistic if it responds more directly to their intentions. Naturalness matters, but it does not automatically improve control, satisfaction, enjoyment, or perceived realism.

### 3. Schmierbach, Limperos, and Woolley, “Feeling the Need for (Personalized) Speed: How Natural Controls and Customization Contribute to Enjoyment of a Racing Game Through Enhanced Immersion”

**Primary source.** This peer-reviewed study reports a 2×2 experiment involving a console racing game. The researchers examined the effects of using a steering-wheel controller and allowing players to customize their vehicle.

The steering-wheel controller and vehicle customization increased enjoyment. These effects were mediated by transportation and the balance between challenge and skill, rather than by a direct relationship between presence and enjoyment. Natural or embodied controls can therefore make a game more engaging without automatically producing stronger immersion or believability.

## Points on which all three studies agree

### Believability depends on perceived coherence, not objective realism

All three studies challenge the idea that realism simply means reproducing physical reality as accurately as possible.

- Pouke et al. found that inaccurate physics could feel more realistic than accurate physics.
- Normoyle and Jörg found that less natural animation could produce better satisfaction and control.
- Schmierbach et al. showed that natural controls can improve enjoyment, but their effects depend on other psychological factors.

The common principle is that players judge interaction quality through expectations, context, and feedback—not through scientific accuracy alone.

### Responsiveness is essential

Normoyle and Jörg directly found that low responsiveness reduced performance and perceived control. Across the three studies, players needed their intended actions to be recognized and translated into understandable outcomes. Natural controls can support engagement, but only when the mapping between intention, input, and result remains clear.

### Interaction quality is shaped by player expectations

Players bring mental models to a game. They expect objects to fall, bounce, and move in familiar ways; they expect character movement to respond promptly to input; and they may feel more connected to a racing game when the controller resembles the action of driving. A believable system therefore gives players feedback that is predictable within the game’s world, even when that feedback is not perfectly realistic.

### Immersion is not identical to enjoyment, presence, realism, or control

The studies distinguish among related but separate outcomes. A control scheme can increase enjoyment without directly increasing presence; accurate physics can increase a sense of scale without being judged more realistic; and natural animation can affect enjoyment without improving perceived realism. The essay should not treat believability, immersion, presence, enjoyment, and perceived control as interchangeable concepts.

## Points where the studies differ or require qualification

### Simplified physics versus natural controls

Pouke et al. emphasize that simplified physics may be preferable when accurate physics violates expectations. Normoyle and Jörg similarly show that less natural animation can be preferable when it improves control. Schmierbach et al., however, find benefits from a more natural controller. This is not necessarily a contradiction: a steering wheel may improve the mapping between player action and game action, while physically accurate object behavior or animation may introduce delays, unfamiliar sensations, or inconvenient control.

### Believability versus enjoyment

Pouke et al. primarily measure perceived realism and expectations. Normoyle and Jörg measure performance, control, satisfaction, enjoyment, and perceived realism. Schmierbach et al. focus especially on enjoyment, transportation, and challenge-skill balance. Because the studies measure different outcomes, they do not establish that the same design choice will improve every aspect of player experience. A system can feel more believable without being more enjoyable, or more enjoyable without producing a stronger sense of presence.

### Physical accuracy versus embodied plausibility

Pouke et al. show that accurate physics can help communicate unusual body scale even when players do not prefer it as the more realistic behavior. This differs from the more conventional natural-control result in Schmierbach et al., where similarity to real-world action improves engagement. The relevant distinctions are:

- **Physical accuracy:** whether the simulation follows real-world laws.
- **Interactional plausibility:** whether the action feels understandable and controllable.
- **Embodied correspondence:** whether the input resembles the action being represented.

These dimensions can reinforce one another, but they can also conflict.

## Currently unknown

- Whether the findings generalize beyond resized VR environments, platform games, and racing games.
- How much responsiveness can be increased before movement begins to feel physically implausible.
- Whether players prefer simplified physics because of learned conventions from games, films, ordinary life, or a combination of all three.
- How genre affects the balance between realistic motion and responsive control.
- Whether natural controls improve long-term immersion or mainly create short-term novelty and enjoyment.
- How different player skill levels affect preferences for realistic versus responsive movement.
- Whether players can learn to accept initially unintuitive physics if the system remains consistent.
- How sound, haptic feedback, animation, camera movement, and visual effects interact with physics and controls.
- Whether embodied controls are more effective for increasing believability, presence, agency, enjoyment, or only transportation.
- How accessibility needs affect the relationship between natural controls and intuitive controls.
- How designers should measure plausibility separately from realism, presence, immersion, enjoyment, and perceived control.

## Synthesis for the future essay

The three studies support a careful design principle: plausible interactions are not necessarily the most physically accurate interactions. They are interactions that respond clearly to player intention, follow consistent rules, match reasonable expectations, and provide feedback that helps the player understand what happened.

For this section, the strongest contrast is between realism as simulation and realism as experience. Players generally need the second: a world whose interactions feel coherent, responsive, and understandable from within the player’s embodied perspective.

# Essay Outline: What Makes an Immersive and Believable Video Game?

## Recommended structure

The essay should contain seven paragraphs:

- one introduction;
- five support paragraphs; and
- one conclusion.

Five support paragraphs are recommended because the thesis includes several distinct dimensions of believability and immersion. If the assignment requires a traditional five-paragraph essay, the atmosphere and interaction sections can be combined into one support paragraph.

## Paragraph 1: Introduction — Defining Believability and Immersion

- Introduce video games as interactive worlds rather than purely visual or narrative media.
- Define **believability** as the sense that a game world behaves coherently according to understandable rules.
- Define **immersion** as the player’s sustained feeling of involvement, presence, and investment.
- Explain that immersion does not depend solely on advanced graphics or photorealistic design.
- Briefly introduce the major factors: internal consistency; meaningful player agency; environmental storytelling and coherent level design; atmosphere and emotional tone; and plausible interactions and intuitive controls.
- Thesis: An immersive and believable video game emerges when its internal rules, player choices, environmental storytelling, level design, emotional atmosphere, and interactive systems work together coherently while giving players understandable opportunities to act and influence the world.

## Paragraph 2: Internal Consistency and World Rules

- Define internal consistency as the reliable relationship among a game’s physics, social systems, narrative rules, objects, and consequences.
- Explain that fictional or fantastical worlds can feel believable if their rules are stable and legible.
- Present evidence from Weines and Borit on completeness, consistency, correspondence, and coherent world systems.
- Present evidence from Cheng and Cairns on players sometimes overlooking inconsistencies when the broader experience remains coherent.
- Address rule legibility, temporal persistence, and the balance between completeness and mystery.
- Explain that players need to understand what rules govern the world and what consequences follow from actions.
- Qualification: complete realism is unnecessary, but unexplained contradictions can weaken belief in the world.

## Paragraph 3: Meaningful Player Agency and Persistent Consequences

- Define meaningful agency as the perception that player decisions produce understandable and important effects.
- Explain that agency differs from merely offering multiple dialogue options or superficial choices.
- Present evidence from research on meaningful choice, player agency, and persistent changes to game states.
- Distinguish actual agency from perceived agency.
- Explain how agency supports immersion: players feel responsible for events, consequences create emotional investment, and future possibilities change because of earlier actions.
- Show why feedback is necessary: the game must communicate what changed, consequences should be attributable to the player’s decision, and outcomes do not always need to be positive to feel meaningful.
- Note the design risk: excessive persistence can create confusion, contradictions, or overwhelming complexity.

## Paragraph 4: Environmental Storytelling and Convincing World/Level Design

- Explain that players interpret spaces, objects, routes, and environmental traces as information.
- Cover **Players interpreting environmental clues**: affordances, readability, landmarks, visual signposting, and player inference.
- Cover **Level design guiding exploration and action**: spatial logic, route choice, world scale, content density, pacing, accessibility, and self-paced discovery.
- Cover **Objects and spaces communicating past events**: decay, reconstruction, abandoned objects, temporal layering, and material evidence.
- Present evidence from Smith Nicholls and Cook on environmental affordances and player interpretation.
- Present evidence from Fernández-Vara on indexical storytelling.
- Present evidence from Hughes et al. on open-world scale, density, connectivity, and self-paced exploration.
- Present evidence from Smith and Worch on arranging environmental clues that make players ask what happened.
- Explain that convincing spaces should feel like places with histories, functions, and relationships rather than collections of disconnected assets.
- Address the tension between guidance and freedom: too little guidance produces confusion, while too much guidance removes discovery.

## Paragraph 5: Environmental Atmosphere and Emotional Tone

- Define atmosphere as the emotional quality that emerges from a coordinated environment.
- Explain that atmosphere is created through lighting, color, sound and silence, weather, environmental motion, visual composition, and pacing.
- Present evidence from Ribeiro et al. on audiovisual thematic cohesion and perceived atmosphere.
- Present evidence from Knez and Niedenthal on lighting’s effects on affect and performance.
- Present evidence from Geslin on color properties and emotional response.
- Explain how atmosphere contributes to immersion by directing attention and shaping emotional expectations.
- Connect atmosphere to appropriate realism: a stylized game can be believable if its visual and audio systems are coherent; photorealism is not required.
- Qualification: atmosphere, immersion, enjoyment, and presence are related but not identical.

## Paragraph 6: Plausible Interactions and Intuitive Controls

- Define plausible interaction as action that responds predictably and meaningfully to player intention.
- Distinguish objective physical realism, expected or intuitive behavior, responsive controls, and embodied correspondence between input and action.
- Present evidence from Pouke et al. on simplified physics sometimes feeling more realistic than accurate physics.
- Present evidence from Normoyle and Jörg on the trade-off between animation naturalness and responsiveness.
- Present evidence from Schmierbach, Limperos, and Woolley on natural controllers, transportation, and enjoyment.
- Explain that players often prefer prompt feedback, predictable responses, understandable physics, and controls that support a clear sense of intention.
- Include the central qualification: realistic movement can reduce believability if it delays or obscures the player’s action.
- Connect the paragraph to agency and immersion: players feel more present when their actions produce clear, coherent effects.

## Paragraph 7: Conclusion — Integrating the Systems

- Restate that immersion and believability are produced by relationships among systems, not by one isolated feature.
- Summarize that consistent and legible world rules create trust.
- Summarize that agency and persistent consequences create investment.
- Summarize that environmental storytelling and level design make spaces interpretable and meaningful.
- Summarize that atmosphere gives the world emotional force.
- Summarize that plausible interactions and intuitive controls connect the player’s intentions to the world.
- Emphasize that realism should be understood as experiential coherence rather than exact imitation of reality.
- Acknowledge the central design challenge: systems must reinforce one another without eliminating ambiguity, freedom, or player interpretation.
- Closing idea: The most immersive and believable games are those in which players can understand the world, act meaningfully within it, interpret its history, feel its atmosphere, and trust that the game will respond coherently to what they do.
