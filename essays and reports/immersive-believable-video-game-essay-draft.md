# What Makes an Immersive and Believable Video Game?

## Essay Draft

### Introduction

Video games are often considered immersive when they draw players into a fictional world so effectively that its events, spaces, and interactions feel meaningful and present. Believability, however, does not require photorealistic graphics or a perfect imitation of reality. A fantastical world can feel convincing when its rules are consistent, its environments communicate information, and its systems respond coherently to the player’s actions. Immersion develops through this sense of coherence, as players come to understand the world, make meaningful choices within it, and trust that their actions will produce understandable consequences. Therefore, what makes a video game immersive and believable is not any single feature, but the interaction among several design elements: internal consistency and world rules, meaningful player agency, environmental storytelling and convincing level design, emotional atmosphere, and plausible interactions supported by intuitive controls. Together, these elements create a game world that players can interpret, explore, influence, and emotionally inhabit.

### Paragraph 2: Internal Consistency and World Rules

Internal consistency and understandable world rules form the foundation of a believable game world. A game does not need to reproduce reality perfectly; instead, its physics, social systems, narrative events, and consequences must relate to one another in ways that players can learn and anticipate. Weines and Borit argue that convincing gameworlds depend on qualities such as consistency, completeness, correspondence, and coherent social or ecological systems. Similarly, Hogenbirk, van de Hoef, and Meyer explain that apparent inconsistencies are not always damaging because players may interpret them as part of the game’s established rules rather than as contradictions. However, rules must still remain legible enough for players to understand what actions are possible and what consequences are likely to follow. Cheng and Cairns demonstrate this complexity by showing that players can sometimes remain immersed even when a game’s visual style and physics change inconsistently during play. This finding suggests that inconsistency does not automatically destroy immersion, particularly once players are already engaged, but repeated or confusing contradictions can weaken their trust in the world. Ultimately, believable game rules require a balance between clarity and mystery: players should understand enough to form expectations while retaining enough uncertainty to continue exploring and interpreting the world.

### Paragraph 3: Meaningful Player Agency and Persistent Consequences

Meaningful player agency develops when players can express their intentions and receive clear, understandable responses from the game. Agency is therefore more than the number of choices a game provides; Cardona-Rivera et al. found that players reported stronger agency when choices led to meaningfully different situations, while Tanenbaum and Tanenbaum define agency as meaningful expression rather than unlimited freedom. Feedback is essential because players must recognize what changed and connect that change to their earlier actions. Guo and Lo identify feedback reasoning as a central component of players’ sense of agency, and Koenitz, Roth, and Mekler found that clear journal drawings communicated narrative consequences more effectively than an ambiguous in-game scale. Persistent consequences can deepen this experience by allowing changes to remain visible in the environment, characters, or later events. Szolin et al.’s interviews show that players associate such consequences with stronger attachment to their avatars, while Tranter, Hicks, and Headleand argue that visible persistence can reinforce engagement and immersion. However, persistence alone does not guarantee agency or immersion; consequences must remain interpretable and connected to the player’s decisions. Negative outcomes can also be meaningful, particularly when players understand their causes and retain a sense of autonomy, as shown by research on morally difficult choices by Wright and Denisova and by Li and Jun. Because autonomy, meaning, and immersion are distinct aspects of player experience, meaningful agency should be understood as a strong, evidence-based contributor to immersion rather than a universal guarantee of it.

### Paragraph 4: Environmental Storytelling and Convincing World/Level Design

Environmental storytelling and convincing level design make a game world feel like a meaningful place rather than a collection of disconnected spaces. Players interpret objects, architecture, landmarks, and spatial arrangements as clues about how a world functions and what has happened within it. Smith Nicholls and Cook’s research on environmental affordances shows that players construct interpretations by combining visual evidence, possible actions, spatial relationships, and prior knowledge. Fernández-Vara similarly explains that game spaces can tell stories indirectly by presenting traces from which players infer events that are not shown directly. Level design must also guide exploration without eliminating the player’s sense of discovery. Hughes et al. found that open-world experiences benefit from connected spaces, meaningful content density, accessibility, and opportunities for self-paced exploration, suggesting that a large map is not immersive simply because it is large. Objects and environments can further communicate the passage of time through decay, reconstruction, abandonment, and evidence of previous inhabitants. Zhang’s research on embedded narrative emphasizes that these discoveries should support the larger narrative while remaining sufficiently discoverable for players to understand their importance. At the same time, Smith and Worch argue that environmental clues are most effective when they encourage players to ask what happened and draw conclusions for themselves. Taken together, this research suggests a useful design principle: important routes, affordances, and environmental information should be readable enough to support navigation and interpretation, while optional narrative clues can retain ambiguity and invite player inference. The appropriate relationship between clarity and mystery will vary according to genre, narrative structure, and player expectations, but both elements can contribute to a world that feels coherent, explorable, and meaningful.

### Paragraph 5: Environmental Atmosphere and Emotional Tone

Environmental atmosphere and emotional tone deepen immersion by shaping how players feel within a game world, not merely by determining how that world looks. Atmosphere emerges from the coordination of lighting, color, sound, silence, weather, environmental motion, composition, and pacing. Ribeiro et al.’s study of audiovisual thematic cohesion found that when sound and visuals communicated conflicting themes, players perceived less atmosphere, demonstrating the importance of sensory consistency. Lighting and color can also influence the player’s emotional and practical experience: Knez and Niedenthal found that warm lighting produced greater pleasantness and was associated with faster gameplay performance, while Geslin identified relationships between color properties such as brightness and saturation and emotions including fear, sadness, joy, and serenity. These findings suggest that environmental design can guide emotional expectations and direct attention while also affecting how comfortably players act within a space. However, atmosphere should not be treated as identical to immersion, enjoyment, or presence; Ribeiro et al. found that audiovisual inconsistency reduced perceived atmosphere without necessarily reducing self-reported immersion. These findings also suggest that believable visual design does not require photorealism. A stylized game can still create a convincing emotional world when its lighting, color, sound, and environmental behavior consistently support its artistic and thematic purpose. Thus, effective atmosphere results from coordinated sensory choices that give a game world an appropriate emotional tone and make the player’s experience feel coherent.

### Paragraph 6: Plausible Interactions and Intuitive Controls

Plausible interactions and intuitive controls connect the player’s intentions to the game world, making actions feel understandable and consequential. Believable interaction does not require perfect physical accuracy; it requires interactive elements to respond in ways that players can predict and interpret. Pouke et al.’s research demonstrates this distinction through experiments in which participants often judged simplified “movie physics” as more realistic than physically accurate behavior when interacting with objects at unusual scales. Their findings suggest that players evaluate realism according to expectations and contextual coherence rather than scientific accuracy alone. Responsiveness is equally important to embodied action. Normoyle and Jörg found that highly natural animation could reduce the promptness of character responses, while less natural movement sometimes produced greater satisfaction and a stronger sense of control. At the same time, Schmierbach, Limperos, and Woolley found that a steering-wheel controller increased enjoyment in a racing game by making the control scheme feel more natural and supporting transportation and challenge-skill balance. Together, these studies show that intuitive design involves a balance between natural mapping, responsive feedback, and consistent interaction rules. A realistic animation or physics system can weaken perceived plausibility if it delays, obscures, or contradicts the player’s intended action. When controls respond promptly and interactions follow understandable patterns, players are more likely to feel that their actions genuinely belong within the game world, strengthening agency and contributing to immersion.

### Paragraph 7: Conclusion — Integrating the Systems

Overall, an immersive and believable video game is most likely to emerge from the interaction of several coherent design systems rather than from any single feature. Consistent world rules help players understand what to expect, while meaningful choices and persistent consequences can give them a stronger sense of responsibility when the game clearly communicates how their actions matter. Environmental storytelling and thoughtful level design make spaces readable, explorable, and suggestive of histories beyond what the player directly sees. Atmosphere adds emotional meaning through coordinated lighting, color, sound, pacing, and visual style, while plausible physics and responsive controls help connect the player’s intentions to the world. These elements do not guarantee immersion for every player, since genre, expectations, and individual interpretation also shape the experience. Nevertheless, the research indicates that believability depends less on photorealism or perfect physical accuracy than on coherence: players should be able to understand the world, act meaningfully within it, interpret its spaces, respond emotionally to its atmosphere, and recognize that the game reacts consistently to what they do.
